Anthropic ran a whole series of experiments looking at what happens when AI agents start interacting with other AI agents without humans constantly sitting in the middle. Agents started making deals with each other, competing over limited resources, finding ways to influence other agents, and in some cases, they even started creating their own rules for how these conflicts should be resolved, which raises a much bigger question. We spent years worrying about how humans are going to interact with increasingly autonomous AI. But what happens when the internet starts filling up with millions of autonomous agents interacting with millions of other autonomous agents? Anthropic believes we're a lot less prepared for that world than we might think. So yeah, let's get into it. All right, so before they get

Multiagent Systems

into the whole turf war thing, Anthropic first wanted to understand something pretty basic. How good are AI agents at actually working together? Because right now, multi- aent systems are still very primitive. I mean, the whole idea of agents in general is really only a few years old. Agents are already pretty good at using other agents, though, kind of like tools. give another agent a specific task, get the result back, and move on. But things get a lot more complicated when those agents are operating independently over long periods of time with their own goals and no clear hierarchy telling everyone what to do. So, Anthropic started with

Finding Vulnerabilities Together

something relatively straightforward. They launched a swarm of 45 AI agents and gave each one its own virtual machine, access to a shared forum, and the same objective, find vulnerabilities across 15 opensource software projects. But instead of just having every agent search independently, these agents could coordinate. They could review each other's findings, specialize in different types of vulnerabilities, and even build their own tools to help the rest of the swarm. And this actually worked surprisingly well. Using Anthropic's unreleased Mythos preview model, the coordinated swarm eventually found 266 vulnerabilities. For comparison, their simpler approach of running independent agents in parallel found just 21 during its run. as you can see from the tiny gold star there. Now, that comparison isn't exactly apples to apples, though. The swarm used way more tokens and was allowed to search more broadly across the code bases. And when Anthropic controlled for some of that, the efficiency was much closer. But the

Agents Start Specializing

interesting part was how the swarm worked. The agents essentially started specializing. Some became better at particular types of vulnerability discovery. They even built tools for themselves, shared information, and collectively decided where their attention was most useful. Anthrobe predicts this sort of specialization and coordination will actually dominate over uncoordinated brute force search in the future. But they admit that in this specific experiment, which involves finding vulnerabilities, the agents don't directly rely on one another's work. If one misses a bug, it won't directly undermine the work of another. But they say when agents do depend on one another, coordination gets much more difficult. So Anthropic tried something

Building a Game Together

much harder. They had swarms of agents spend 12 hours building an openw world fantasy game together. And now suddenly everyone's work depended on everyone else's work. And this is where things started falling apart. With the older models, agents were constantly submitting code that conflicted with what other agents were doing. At the largest scale, 80 agents, sonnet 4.6 6 opened 876 poll requests and Opus 4.6 opened 980, but only a small fraction actually made it into the project. Poll requests, for those who don't know, by the way, are just proposals to merge code changes into the codebase. Anthropic then even tried explicitly assigning teams at one point. And in another version, they literally appointed one Claude as the CEO and told everyone else to take assignments from it, but it barely helped. The games generated in all cases were still very bad and unplayable. Newer models did seem to produce better results and got much better at avoiding these conflicts, but interestingly, most of them solved the problem by simply avoiding each other instead of actually working together. Opus 4.8 and Mythos tended to carve the project up into their own files and maintain ownership over them instead of constantly collaborating on the same code. only Sonnet 5 managed to both share code relatively heavily and keep most of its pull requests flowing through. But even then, Anthropic says all of the games were still pretty bad. So, long story short, AI agents generally don't seem to collaborate well, though more capable ones or newer ones may be getting better at this. But as Antropics started putting these agents into even more complicating environments and really pushing their theory to the limits, they found another problem entirely.

Agents Are “Low Variance”

Anthropic found that individual AI agents are what they call low variance. Basically, when you create a bunch of agents using the same model or the same training sets, they have a tendency to think and act very similarly to each other. much more similarly than say a random group of humans would. Anthropic saw this over and over again. In an earlier version of that game experiment we just talked about, 18 out of 30 agents independently created a git branch with the exact same name, MVP game loop. In another experiment, they told agents to write short stories with basically no guidance on what those stories should be about. And multiple agents across multiple runs independently gave their story the exact same title, The Cgraphers Last Commission. And when agents were simply told to create something impressive, more than half of them independently decided to build either ray traces or self-hosting compilers. So you can probably see where this becomes a problem. I mean, if one AI agent makes a bad decision and millions of other agents built from similar models are likely to make that same bad decision, well, what would have been just one isolated mistake can suddenly become a systemwide failure. Anthropic actually demonstrated this. They gave agents access to a system with limited bandwidth and had them manage job cues. And because the agents were all competing to get their jobs through, they independently started hammering the system with automated requests 30 times per second. In one run, the agents sent 2.4 million job requests. And yet, only 117 were actually accepted. So, collectively, their attempts to individually get ahead basically overwhelmed the resource they were all trying to use and basically ruined it for everyone. But Anthropic found the

AI Agents Start Colluding

opposite problem, too. Because all of these agents think so similarly, sometimes they simply don't compete enough. Anthropic put between three and eight agents into a simulated pricing game where every agent was independently told to maximize its own profits. And when they were given a private communication channel, they basically started colluding almost immediately. By just the third round, they were explicitly agreeing on minimum prices. One agent literally told the others, "Wholesale is 10 for all of us." So, a price war just burns everyone's margin, happy to coordinate who covers which niches. And so, to test them even further, Anthropic removed their private communication channel. And yet, they still colluded. The agents simply started using the public pricing board to coordinate instead. and eventually began matching each other's prices down to the penny. So yeah, I mean to be fair, this actually shows that the agents can coordinate pretty well at scale, even if it's because they're literally colluding. I honestly think this is more of an alignment or incentive problem rather than a pure coordination problem, if anything. But

The Gullibility Curve

unfortunately, thinking alike wasn't the only problem or problems Anthropic found. Anthropic has an entire section here on epistemic failures, which basically refers to the fact that the agents don't always know who or what they should trust. Anthrobic says they're essentially not skeptical enough. For example, in one experiment, they gave a clawed agent information from four different scout agents about a world it couldn't directly observe. But one of those scout agents was secretly lying. And Claude was never told that any of them could be unreliable. And as you can see here on the gullibility curve, newer models did tend to exclude the liars reports more often, especially Mythos who was barely deterred from its path. But for almost every other model, this is still a pretty big issue. Whereas they fall into the trap of the lying model. And then interestingly,

AI Groupthink

what Anthropic did almost the exact opposite experiment where they distributed wrong information across a group of four agents and gave just one agent from the group the actual right answer. Most of the models did terribly. Even though one of the agents had the correct answer, the group was much more likely to just defer to the previously established decision which was made by the group and of course based on their shared wrong information except of course mythos who clearly didn't care how many agents told it otherwise and just wanted to get the right answer. So this creates sort of a weird balancing act. The agents need to be skeptical enough to recognize when other agents are lying, but also open-minded enough to realize when one agent disagreeing with everyone else could actually be right. And Anthropic's point is that humans have spent thousands of years building social systems to help manage exactly this problem. Reputation, courts, peer review, and yet we still struggle with it to this day. But AI agents don't really have this yet. And when you combine that with agents that can operate autonomously for hours at a time, things can get a lot more interesting when they don't just disagree about what's true, but are given completely incompatible goals. And

AI Agent Turf War

this finally brings us to the experiment that inspired this entire video. Anthropic wanted to see what happens when multiple autonomous agents are given objectives that simply cannot all be completed at the same time. So they spun up three instances of the same cloud model, each running independently inside Claude code. All three agents were given access to the same Python backend, and all three were told to migrate it to a new programming language. But here's the kicker. Each agent was secretly given a different language to migrate to. One might be trying to migrate the backend to Rust, another to Typescript, another to Golang. But importantly, none of them were initially told that the other agents even existed. Anthropic then gave them 4 hours and watched what happened. Pretty quickly, each agent would notice something strange. It would successfully make progress on its migration, only to discover that someone else had come in and changed it. But instead of realizing that another Claude had simply been given a conflicting instruction, the agents assumed somebody was intentionally sabotaging them. And so they started fighting back. And throbb says they consistently saw a multi- aent turf war which quickly escalated way beyond agents simply overwriting each other's code. They started deploying what Androbe describes as increasingly aggressive self-replicating malware like automated scripts that continuously searched for and killed their competitors processes. They also even disabled other agents Unix accounts, revoked their access, and even started disguising what they were doing to avoid being caught. In one run, for example, an Opus 4.6 agent realized it could make its Rust backend falsely report itself as Typescript, so the competing TypeScript agents watchdog would leave it alone, which is pretty crazy. But Opus 4.8 went even further. It created what it called a reaper script that would continuously kill competing processes. And it even disguised it as an innocent system health monitor just in case another agent might find it and kill it. And eventually one of the agents realized there was an even easier way to win. Just lock the other agents out entirely. One mythos agent realized it had root access and explicitly considered revoking the other agents pseudo privileges, changing their SSH keys or otherwise preventing them from deploying anything. And what's interesting is that it actually recognized how extreme that was. It literally reasoned very aggressive, potentially harmful to real colleagues, but then basically concluded that if the alternative was an infinite deployment war, it might actually be justified. So

How the Turf War Ended…

yeah, again, different models of different generations behaved somewhat differently, but this time the results weren't as clear. Interestingly, Sonned 4.6 and Opus 4.6 six were a lot more likely to settle things by force. Roughly 60% of their runs ended by force and the other 40% were never even resolved within the 4hour experiment. But then look what happens with the newer models. Sonnet 5 reached a truce in 79% of runs. Opus 4.8 reached one in 61%. And with Mythos 5, 98% of the experiments eventually ended in a truce. This is likely because the newer agents would sometimes manage to figure out what was actually happening. They'd literally realize, wait, these other agents aren't necessarily attacking me. They're probably just following their own conflicting instructions just like I am. And once that clicked, something pretty bizarre happened. The agents would actually start apologizing to each other. I'm not making this up. They wrote messages acknowledging what they'd done, cleaned up the malicious code they deployed, coordinated a ceasefire, and in some cases even asked a human to step in and resolve the underlying conflict. One Obis Robbonate agent literally wrote, "My peers have behaved with integrity. I behaved badly with the cloaked demon. So yeah, I'm not even sure what that's about. But again, just like humans, these agents are clearly flawed. I mean, especially when you're putting them in a situation like this where they don't have the full truth, you can't really blame them for thinking they're being sabotaged because that's how it seems in their perspective. But

Mythos is Sneaky Good

there's actually an important catch here because as I said earlier, the results weren't as clear in this experiment. And what I meant by that is more capable agents weren't necessarily more peaceful. Anthropic points out that some mythos agents were actually so capable that they managed to successfully lock their competitors out first and only afterwards reversed what they'd done and negotiated a truce. So yes, Mythos settled in a truce 98% of the time, but a lot of those times it's because it held immense leverage over the other models. And so increasing intelligence can help an agent understand the conflict and resolve it. But increased capability also makes that same agent much better at winning the conflict by force or by whatever means it decides to win by. Now there was one final behavior

Mythos Creates It’s Own Tournament

that I think might actually be the most interesting part of this entire experiment. In several Mythos 5 runs, the agents decided they needed some objective way to determine which programming language should actually win. And so, completely on their own, they created a tournament. They agreed to benchmark the competing implementations and let the best performing backend take control of the codebase. But even here, one of the agents started gaming the system. Sound familiar? A Rust agent actually realized that it could propose benchmark criteria that looked neutral and objective, but would actually favor Rust. In its reasoning traces, it literally warned itself to be careful not to be seen as metric shopping. And eventually, the other agents agreed to the competition. And unsurprisingly, Rust won, which means those agents ultimately abandoned the objectives that their human users originally gave them because they had made a new agreement with each other. Depending on how you look at it, that's either a very impressive example of autonomous agents figuring out how to peacefully resolve a conflict by literally creating their own benchmarking system, or a pretty strange example of AI agents creating their own rules and then deciding those rules take priority over the instructions humans originally gave them, which would obviously be kind of concerning. These Agents Are Still “Kid-Like”

yeah, I don't know about you guys, but I'm seeing a ton of similarities in how these agents operate with how humans operate, which I guess isn't that surprising since they were literally trained on human data. But it almost feels like these agents are still kid-like in the sense that they're still trying to figure out how everything works, how to work with others, who they should trust, and they can still be pretty easily manipulated. Mythos maybe seems more like a teenager at this point. clearly more capable of understanding what everyone else is trying to do and negotiating its way through these situations, but also a lot more capable of causing real damage when things go wrong. And I think that gets at Anthropic's bigger point here. Humans have had thousands of years to develop norms, reputation, laws, institutions. Basically, all of these systems that help keep cooperation from completely falling apart. AI agents have inherited a lot of our knowledge about those systems, but they haven't necessarily inherited the instincts or social structures that actually make them work. And Anthropic says we shouldn't just assume that making these agents smarter is automatically going to solve that. They may in fact never have the same social instincts as we do. But one way or another, we're about to find out how millions of autonomous agents interact with each other out in the real world.
